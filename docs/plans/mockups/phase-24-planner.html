Drawing, More and Done

Builtin #302 and #342

1 · The toolbox: how the next leg is drawn

Decided: one drop-down on phones; the labelled toolbar with tooltips on desktops. Two different ideas, two places. The drawing mode is a tool: it decides how the next leg you draw is made, and leaves the drawn legs alone (a leg's own mode is changed by tapping it on the map, as today). The route style is a setting: it decides which ways Follow paths prefers, for the whole route, and changing it routes the route again. So the mode is the button, and the style is its second line, shown only for the modes it applies to; in the menu the modes are a list with what each does, and the styles a set of chips under "Route style · the whole route".

  • Phones: one button with the mode's icon and name (never an icon alone), undo, redo and More (⋯), floating at the bottom left above the sheet. Where it can't clear the map's buttons (narrow phones, long words, 200 % zoom) or with the sheet raised high, it docks into the sheet as its first row instead. In the route's view only More stays.
  • Desktops: the toolbar hangs off the panel's edge, one surface with it, so it reads as part of planning, not another floating panel. The three modes with their names, the style as its own labelled control, undo and redo; then the route's actions: Reverse, Back to start and Show the whole route as icons with tooltips; then More and Done. Tooltips give the key and one sentence; keyboard focus shows them too. With less room, Reverse and Back to start fold into More (they're there anyway), then the style's label goes. In the route's view the toolbar keeps Show the whole route, More and Edit.
  • States: the mode in use is filled and ticked, not only coloured; undo and redo show from the first change and are disabled (not hidden) when there's nothing to undo or redo. At the start there's nothing to show but the mode. Changed 2026-10-20: the toolbox grows with the route. No toolbox before the first point (only undo and redo, while there's something to undo or redo); the first point brings the mode and More; the first leg the route's actions, where there's room. The frames here follow that.

3 · More (⋯), for the whole route

Decided: More (⋯), familiar, compact, and not tied to one kind of action. It's the last button of the toolbox on phones (beside the drawing tools, not in the route's summary) and of the toolbar on desktops; in the route's view it stays there, the drawing tools gone. Grouped by what it's for: take it along (a share link, a GPX file, the map saved for no signal), the whole route (a round trip from the start, reverse, back to start, a file to plan on), and Clear route apart, in red, after a gap, with Undo. Its head says the route is kept on this device. What's also somewhere else, on purpose: Take it along in Go, Reverse and Back to start in Route's tools (and the desktop toolbar), Import at the start. Moving the panel stays with the panel (22b).

4 · Done, the route's view, and coming back

As built in 25c. Done ends editing. The route stays open to read, check and take along: this is the route's view. A tap on the map shows what's there instead of adding a point. Edit, where Done was, goes back to drawing. ✕ puts the planner away, and Plan opens it again as it was left. Nothing is lost: the route is kept on this device, and undo outlives Done.

Today, with the sheet at its peek, nothing says there's anything to read before you go: Check's count is on its tab, under the fold, and a row in Check frames the map without marking the place (#84, #85). 5 · Check: how serious, and how sure

A count alone doesn't say whether to worry. Check sorts what's already found into three groups, each with its own icon, word and shape (never colour alone): Read before you go (what can hurt, stop you or turn you back: shipping lanes, rough water, ice, the dark, road works, no access), Worth a look (sections to know about: steep, technical, carries, steps, ways marked private, trail details), and Not checked (a source that didn't answer, an estimate standing in). Each item says where it comes from: live, from the map (may be wrong), an estimate, or calculated. The end of Check says what was looked at, and that nothing listed doesn't mean open, safe or allowed. The tab's badge and a row in the peek follow the most serious group. Nothing new is checked: these are today's Heads-up items, sorted.

E · Each sport's key facts (A2, chosen 2026-10-24)

The strip that C removes held each sport's own facts, not only the hardest climb. For paddling it shows Headwind and Strongest wind, and Carry ascent; for MTB, gravel and road the hardest climb; for walking and running the high point. C gave a new place only to Speed, Style and the hardest climb. The Key facts switch at the bottom shows where the rest could go; the first round's frames are under earlier versions. A2, chosen 2026-10-24: one line under the route's numbers, always in sight, even at the peek, showing what stands out on this route, else the sport's usual fact. A tap opens where it's told in full, and Check's top keeps them all. A: the same line with one fact chosen by the sport alone (the first round). B: at the top of Check, as the strip is today, without Speed and Style. C: a fourth number beside the route's own.

What stands out (A2). The three numbers already say how hard the route is: the time counts the climbs, the surface, a bike's headwind and your speed. So the line says why, or what could make the day differ from the numbers. It shows the first of these that stands out: what can change whether you go (a water leg's wind, always; rain on the way), then what changes how long (a headwind that adds 15 min or a tenth of the time), then the hardest bit (unpaved on a road bike, a trail grade above the route's style, a climb steep enough to walk). When nothing does, it shows the sport's usual fact: the hardest climb on a bike, the high point on foot. The thresholds are proposals. Weather that can't be judged yet is said beside it, never taken as nothing standing out. A route of two sports gets a row for each, since its totals mix them: the sport, its distance and its own fact (its climbing, or a water leg's wind); a sport with two legs shows them together, and the list has each leg. A tap opens that part in the list. A trip of several days names its hardest day.
